I · Orientation

How to read Sanātana Dharma

There is no single book or institution that exhausts the tradition. Start by understanding its layers, vocabulary and diverse lineages.

Śruti · “that which is heard”

Traditionally, the Vedas are classified as śruti: revealed sacred literature preserved through rigorous oral transmission. The Saṃhitās, Brāhmaṇas, Āraṇyakas and Upaniṣads form major textual layers of Vedic literature.

Textual foundation

Smṛti · “that which is remembered”

Smṛti encompasses a broad family of remembered and transmitted literature, including Dharmaśāstra, Itihāsa, Purāṇa and many later traditions. Its authority and role vary by school and context.

Expansive literature

Sampradāya · living transmission

A sampradāya is a lineage of teaching and practice. Vaiṣṇava, Śaiva, Śākta, Smārta and many regional traditions preserve distinct theological languages while sharing a wider civilizational vocabulary.

Lineage matters

Today’s learning trail

1 · Learn a term

Dharma does not map neatly to one English word; context can include duty, order, right conduct, law or the sustaining principle of a situation.

2 · Locate the source

Ask: Veda, Upaniṣad, Gītā, Purāṇa, Āgama, epic, philosophical sūtra or later commentary?

3 · Compare interpretations

Different schools can read the same passage differently. Difference is part of the intellectual history, not necessarily an error.

III · Deep archive preview

What lies below the surface

Why are there so many deities?
Hindu traditions contain multiple theological models: devotion to a chosen form (iṣṭa-devatā), traditions centered on Viṣṇu, Śiva or Devī, philosophical accounts of ultimate reality, and regional forms. The existence of many forms does not imply one uniform Hindu theology; it reflects a plural religious civilization.
Why does the same festival differ by region?
Calendars, lunar months, local ritual manuals, temple customs, regional histories and sampradāya practices can produce legitimate variation. A responsible portal should label the regional convention instead of presenting one observance as universally identical.
What makes a source “primary”?
For study purposes, a primary text is a source directly belonging to the tradition under investigation, such as a Sanskrit text or a historically situated inscription. A translation is a representation of that source; a commentary is an interpretation; a modern article is secondary scholarship.

Curator’s method

Do not flatten living traditions

Name, form and theology are not identical
A divine name may have Vedic, epic, Purāṇic, Āgamic, Tantric, regional and philosophical histories. Record the form being discussed, the source where it appears and the tradition interpreting it before drawing a universal conclusion.
Iconography is evidence of a visual convention, not a single universal code
Attributes such as śaṅkha, cakra, triśūla, lotus, vāhana and mudrā can identify forms, but their interpretation depends on iconographic manuals, regional sculpture, temple practice and the specific deity image.
Avatāra is a technical theological category
In Vaiṣṇava traditions, avatāra theology develops in different texts and schools. Do not automatically treat every popular list or regional divine figure as an identical textual category.
Primary-source trail
For every deity dossier, the production version should attach source layers: Vedic references where relevant; epic passages; Purāṇic passages; Āgama/Tantra where applicable; stotra or devotional literature; inscriptional/art-historical evidence; and modern scholarship.

Names are contextual

“Śiva”, “Rudra”, “Maheśvara”, “Śaṅkara” and many epithets overlap but are not interchangeable in every textual or theological context. Study the passage, lineage and period.

Myth is layered

Purāṇic narratives often exist in multiple versions. A difference between sources can be historically meaningful. Record the source instead of silently merging variants.

Study discipline

A five-pass reading method

Pass 1 · Read the passage

Do not begin by searching for a modern quote. Understand the immediate speaker, listener and situation.

Pass 2 · Locate the vocabulary

Identify terms such as ātman, dharma, yoga, brahman, prakṛti or mokṣa and keep their technical meaning visible.

Pass 3 · Compare translations

Compare at least two responsible translations and note where choices differ.

Pass 4 · Read a commentary

Commentaries reveal how a particular school builds an interpretation from the text.

Pass 5 · Reflect without rewriting the source

Personal application is valuable, but label it as application rather than claiming the ancient text explicitly says something it does not.

Deep archive

How the textual layers fit together

Saṃhitā

Collections of mantras, hymns or ritual formulas. In Vedic study, preserve the distinction between the text itself and the ritual/philological interpretation attached later.

Brāhmaṇa

Prose Vedic literature explaining ritual procedures, meanings and correspondences. It is not simply a commentary in the modern sense.

Āraṇyaka

“Forest” texts associated with transitions between ritual and speculative modes of interpretation. Boundaries between textual layers are historically complex.

Upaniṣadic layer

Texts concerned with knowledge, self, reality, ritual reinterpretation and liberation. “The Upaniṣads” are a large and diverse corpus, not one uniform philosophical book.

Sūtra & śāstra

Compact aphoristic texts and systematic treatises often require a commentary tradition to unpack technical arguments.

Commentary

Bhāṣya, ṭīkā and other commentarial genres preserve intellectual debates. A commentary should always be labeled by author, school and period.

Edition discipline
A production scripture reader should store edition, recension where applicable, language, editor/translator, publication details and passage identifier. This prevents a later UI from presenting a paraphrase as if it were a critical text.
Translation discipline
Translations are interpretive acts. Where a Sanskrit term is technically important, show the original term and explain the translation choice rather than silently replacing it with one English equivalent.
Commentary discipline
When presenting a traditional commentary, identify its author and school. Do not merge Śaṅkara, Rāmānuja, Madhva or modern teachers into one anonymous “Hindu interpretation.”

The five limbs

Pañcāṅga, explained precisely

1 · Tithi

A lunar day defined by the angular separation of the Moon and Sun. There are 30 tithis in a synodic lunar cycle; each spans 12° of separation. Tithis are grouped into śukla and kṛṣṇa pakṣa.

2 · Vāra

The weekday: Ravi-vāra (Sunday), Soma-vāra (Monday), Maṅgala-vāra (Tuesday), Budha-vāra (Wednesday), Guru-vāra (Thursday), Śukra-vāra (Friday), Śani-vāra (Saturday).

3 · Nakṣatra

The ecliptic is divided into 27 equal sectors of 13°20′. The Moon’s sidereal position is conventionally associated with one nakṣatra; traditions can use specific ayanāṃśa conventions.

4 · Yoga

In the pañcāṅga sense, yoga is calculated from the sum of the Sun and Moon’s longitudes, divided into 27 sectors of 13°20′. This is distinct from the philosophical system of Yoga.

5 · Karaṇa

A half-tithi, corresponding to 6° of Sun–Moon angular separation. The karaṇa system contains repeating and fixed components with specific calendrical rules.

Why sunrise matters

Traditional day-bound observances often use local sunrise as a boundary. Therefore a festival cannot safely be assigned by simply looking at a midnight-to-midnight civil calendar date.

Festival atlas

Major observance families

Dīpāvalī

A festival complex associated with lamps, prosperity, renewal and several regional narratives. Lakṣmī pūjā, Govardhana Pūjā, Bhāi Dūj and other observances can occupy different days within the wider period.

Navarātri

A nine-night festival period with major regional forms such as Durgā Pūjā in Bengal, Garbā traditions in Gujarat and Rāmlīlā-linked observances in North India.

Mahāśivarātri

A major Śaiva observance associated with Śiva. Ritual details vary, including fasting, night vigil and abhiṣeka traditions.

Janmāṣṭamī

Celebrates Kṛṣṇa’s birth in Vaiṣṇava traditions. Timing and midnight observance rules can differ by calendar and location.

Rāma Navamī

Associated with the birth of Rāma. The observance is tied to a specific tithi and commonly includes worship, recitation and temple celebrations.

Holi

A spring festival complex with regional ritual and social forms. Holikā Dahana and the following day’s color celebrations are distinct components.

Precision note

A good calendar tells you what it knows

Important: astronomical tithi/nakṣatra/yoga values and a temple’s official festival calendar are related but not identical. This interface uses astronomical calculations as an educational layer. For a real ritual observance, follow the calendar published by the relevant temple, lineage or regional pañcāṅga authority.

BUILT SACRED SPACE

How to Read a Hindu Temple

Garbha-gṛha

The sanctum or “womb chamber” containing the principal deity, image or emblem. It forms the ritual centre of many temple plans.

Maṇḍapa

A pillared hall used for gatherings, ritual activity, music, processions and other temple functions.

Śikhara / Vimāna

The superstructure associated with the sanctum. Terminology and architectural forms vary significantly between regions.

Prākāra

Enclosure walls and precincts that organize movement and different levels of sacred space.

Gopura

Monumental gateway towers particularly prominent in large South Indian temple complexes.

Pradakṣiṇā

Ritual circumambulation around a sacred object or space, commonly performed clockwise in many Hindu traditions.

PILGRIMAGE INTELLIGENCE

A Yātrā Is More Than a Route

Ritual Dimension

Pilgrimage may involve darśana, bathing, vows, offerings, circumambulation, recitation, fasting or service. Exact practices depend on the temple and tradition.

Geographical Dimension

Sacred geography connects temples with rivers, mountains, pilgrimage routes, sacred towns and regional networks.

Social Dimension

Pilgrimage historically connects communities, markets, monasteries, festivals, hospitality systems and local economies.

Living Tradition

A temple is not only an architectural monument. It may remain an active centre of worship, festivals, community activity and cultural transmission.

Philosophy & Yoga

Study the systems as arguments: what is real, what is the self, what causes bondage, what counts as knowledge and what constitutes liberation.

Six darśanasCore termsYogaPathsCompare
Classical systems

The six Āstika darśanas

Nyāya

Develops a detailed theory of reasoning and valid cognition (pramāṇa), including perception, inference, comparison and testimony in classical formulations.

Vaiśeṣika

Analyzes categories of reality (padārtha) and developed an influential account of substances, qualities, motion and particulars.

Sāṃkhya

Classically distinguishes puruṣa (consciousness) and prakṛti (nature), explaining experience and liberation through discriminative knowledge.

Yoga

Patañjali’s Yoga system shares Sāṃkhya vocabulary while presenting a disciplined path involving ethics, concentration, meditation and samādhi.

Mīmāṃsā

Analyzes Vedic ritual, dharma, language and methods of knowing, especially in relation to the authority and interpretation of Vedic injunctions.

Vedānta

Centers on the Upaniṣadic, Brahma-sūtra and Gītā corpus, with major schools including Advaita, Viśiṣṭādvaita and Dvaita offering different accounts of Brahman, self and liberation.

Vocabulary engine

Terms that change meaning with context

Dharma
A context-sensitive term covering duty, law, ethical order, right conduct and the sustaining order of a situation. Its meaning must be established from the text or philosophical system rather than assumed from one English gloss.
Karma
At a broad level, karma means action. In philosophical traditions it can also refer to causally significant action and its consequences. Karma should not be reduced to a simplistic “instant cosmic reward/punishment” formula.
Mokṣa
Liberation from saṃsāra, described differently across traditions. Advaita, Viśiṣṭādvaita, Dvaita, Yoga and other systems do not all define the liberated state in the same metaphysical terms.
Ātman
Often translated as self or inner self. Its precise philosophical status differs across traditions, and in some Vedāntic debates its relationship to Brahman is central.
Brahman
In Upaniṣadic and Vedāntic discourse, Brahman can denote ultimate reality. Different Vedānta schools interpret its relation to attributes, world and individual selves in substantially different ways.

Bhagavad Gītā lens

Different yogas, one dialogue

Karma Yoga

Disciplined action, duty and relinquishment of attachment to results. It is not mere busyness; intention, knowledge and discipline matter.

Jñāna Yoga

A path of knowledge and discernment, especially concerned with the nature of self and reality in the Gītā’s philosophical discourse.

Bhakti Yoga

Devotion and loving orientation toward the divine. The Gītā develops devotion alongside knowledge and disciplined action.

Deep philosophy archive

The argument map behind a darśana

Pramāṇa · means of knowledge

Different systems debate which means produce valid cognition. Perception, inference and testimony are central categories; exact lists and their interpretation vary by school.

Prameya · what is known

Philosophical systems identify domains of reality or objects of knowledge. A study should keep the epistemological question distinct from the metaphysical conclusion.

Bandha · bondage

Systems explain bondage differently: ignorance, mistaken identification, karmic entanglement or other conditions. The proposed diagnosis shapes the proposed path to liberation.

Sādhana · means

Knowledge, meditation, disciplined action, devotion and ethical practice are combined differently across traditions. Avoid turning one school's sādhanā into a universal prescription.

Mokṣa · liberation

“Liberation” is a translation label. The metaphysical state described by each school must be studied on its own terms.

Darśana as a method

The word can mean a philosophical “view” or system. The six classical systems are not six denominations and should not be treated as a single ladder of spiritual advancement.

How to compare schools fairly
Use the same questions for each school: What exists? How do we know it? What is the self? What causes bondage? What is liberation? What practices lead there? Then record each school's answer before judging similarities.
Why Yoga is more than postures
Patañjali's Yoga includes ethical disciplines, observances, posture, breath regulation, sensory withdrawal, concentration, meditation and samādhi. Modern physical yoga is related to this history but is not identical to the complete classical system.

Version every important claim
A living portal should store who reviewed a claim, when it was reviewed, what source was used and what changed. This turns “content” into auditable knowledge rather than anonymous text.
Regionalization should be first-class data
Region, language, sampradāya and temple affiliation should be data fields, not buried inside prose. This allows the same concept to be displayed with the right local context.
Search should understand synonyms
A production search index should connect variants such as Kṛṣṇa/Krishna, Śiva/Shiva, Gaṇeśa/Ganesh and Pañcāṅga/Panchang while retaining the canonical scholarly spelling.
False precision
Do not label a regional festival date, ritual sequence or astronomical approximation as universally exact. State the convention and location.
Quote without source
A Sanskrit-looking sentence is not evidence. Store source, chapter/verse or edition information wherever possible.
Pan-Hindu generalization
If a practice belongs specifically to a Śaiva, Vaiṣṇava, Śākta, Smārta or regional context, say so.
Myth/history confusion
